# The Falkenberg Data Center Decision

## A Multi-Stakeholder Siting Conflict over Digital Infrastructure

> **Pre-reading:** Read this briefing and the Key Facts & Reference sheet before the simulation session.

---

## Case Overview

The town of Falkenberg (about 24,000 inhabitants, on the river Verla, in a fictional EU member state) faces the largest investment decision in its history. NimbusData, one of the world's five largest cloud and AI companies, wants to build "Project Aurora", a hyperscale data center campus on 55 hectares of former farmland at the edge of town. Phase 1 is ready to file: 120 megawatts of IT capacity, roughly €1.2 billion of investment, a two-year build. An option for expansion to 450 MW sits in the background, discussed by everyone and confirmed by no one.

The case is fictional, but it is distilled from real, documented European siting conflicts of 2021 to 2026: a contested hyperscale project in Upper Austria, Ireland's grid-capacity crisis and its conditional exit, the collapse of Meta's Zeewolde project in the Netherlands, Frankfurt's chronic scarcity, the Nordic waste-heat successes, and the water conflicts of Aragón. Every stakeholder in this simulation holds a hand that real actors have played, and every side's strongest arguments have an empirical basis.

The decision is open. Comparable projects have been built, modified beyond recognition, and cancelled outright. What decided them was rarely a referendum. It was coalition-building, permit conditions, procedural leverage, and timing.

## The Project

| Parameter | Phase 1 (filed) | Phase 2 (option) |
|-----------|-----------------|------------------|
| IT capacity | 120 MW | up to 450 MW total |
| Investment | ~€1.2 billion | additional billions (unspecified) |
| Site | 55 ha at the Verla industrial zone | same site, denser build-out |
| Construction | ~2 years, ~800 workers on site | n/a |
| Electricity | up to 1.0 TWh/year | would roughly double the region's demand |
| Water (as filed) | up to 600,000 m³/year from the Verla | unknown |
| Permanent jobs | "up to 150" (NimbusData figure) | n/a |

**The site.** The 55 hectares were zoned industrial 15 years ago, when a previous council courted a logistics investor who never came. The zoning has sat dormant since. Legally, no rezoning is required. That fact shapes the entire conflict, because much of the town experiences the project as a new decision while the law treats it as an old one. 40 hectares are under option through the Regional Development Agency. The remaining 15 hectares still belong to two farming families: the Brandts (9 ha) and the Kellers (6 ha), whose farmhouse sits 300 meters from the planned halls. Without those 15 hectares, Phase 1 shrinks to about 80 MW and Phase 2 becomes impossible.

**Why Falkenberg?** The town sits on a major fiber route, 4 km from a transmission substation, with river water for cooling and, above all, pre-zoned land in a Europe where grid-connection queues in the established hubs run 7 to 10 years. NimbusData has said publicly that the region won an internal site competition. It has not said against whom, and a shortlisted alternative site in another region remains warm.

## The Contested Dimensions

**Electricity.** Phase 1 alone would consume up to 1.0 TWh per year, roughly the consumption of 250,000 households, in a region that consumes 3.5 TWh in total. The regional grid can absorb 150 MW without reinforcement; anything beyond requires a new 220-kV line costing about €180 million with a lead time of 6 to 8 years. Who pays for grid expansion, the developer or all electricity customers through network fees, is one of the least resolved questions in European energy regulation.

**Water.** The filed permit requests up to 600,000 m³/year of withdrawal from the Verla, with heated discharge capped at 28 °C. The river's ecological guideline for summer low-flow is 26 °C. The farmers' irrigation association holds senior water rights of 450,000 m³/year. An alternative closed-loop cooling design would cut consumption to about 80,000 m³/year, at roughly €60 million additional cost and slightly lower energy efficiency. Real precedents run in both directions: courts have forced cooling redesigns, and companies have delivered them.

**Jobs.** NimbusData announces "up to 150 permanent jobs" plus around 800 construction workers for 18 months. Independent benchmarks put direct operations staffing at hyperscale facilities at 0.2 to 0.3 full-time positions per MW, which means 25 to 40 people for Phase 1. The gap between those numbers is not necessarily a lie; it depends on whether contractors, security, and facility services count. The most rigorous independent study of the sector finds real but modest local employment effects, and finds that the industry's own impact estimates overstate them roughly threefold.

**Municipal finances.** Property tax of about €1.2 million per year and a payroll-linked local tax of €90,000 to €250,000 per year. That is real money for a town this size, but thin relative to the project's scale, because EU municipalities, unlike their US counterparts, cannot tax server equipment. There is no European precedent for a US-style negotiated community payment. What European municipalities and regulators extract instead are binding permit conditions.

**Waste heat.** Data centers reject heat at 30 to 40 °C, too cold for a legacy district-heating network without heat pumps. Falkenberg has such a network: Stadtwerke Falkenberg serves 2,800 households with gas-fired heat, and the network's edge lies 3.2 km from the site, right at the distance where waste-heat economics usually break. Upgrading the network with pipes and heat pumps would cost about €35 million and could cover around 60% of its demand, replacing most of the gas. Under EU law, NimbusData must analyze waste-heat use. Nobody is obliged to fund it. Projects elsewhere have delivered exactly this, and projects elsewhere have died on exactly this gap.

**Procedure.** National law does not list data centers as a category requiring an environmental impact assessment, while every large wind turbine needs one. The citizens' initiative calls this a regulatory gap; legally, it is settled. The one formal lever open to residents and farmers is party status in the state water-permit hearing.

## Institutional Setting

The permit architecture has three gates, held by three different actors:

1. **Municipal building permit.** The council's formal lever, constrained by the 15-year-old zoning
2. **State water and operating permits.** The State Permitting Authority can attach binding, legally reviewable conditions: efficiency standards, renewable-sourcing obligations, water caps, waste-heat readiness, disclosure duties
3. **Grid connection.** The Regional Grid Operator's technical assessment is a de-facto veto; in the European record, connection policy has stopped more data centers than any planning board

Two institutions shape the game without sitting at the table. The National Energy Authority, the federal energy regulator, oversees the Grid Operator's tariffs and connection policy and ultimately decides who may pay for grid expansion; its published frameworks are the reference the Grid Operator negotiates from. And politics: the state government (represented in this simulation by the State Secretary chairing the conference) controls funding programs and investment promotion and can propose changes to the law, but it cannot lawfully instruct the Permitting Authority how to decide a pending case. The telecom regulator, in case you wondered, plays no role at all. Data centers are not regulated telecommunications infrastructure; their fiber is a commercial contract.

This is the European deal space: not negotiated community checks, but regulatory conditionality. Ireland ended its de-facto connection moratorium by requiring on-site generation, majority sourcing from newly built renewables, and annual disclosure. Germany wrote efficiency ceilings and waste-heat quotas into law. The question in Falkenberg is not only whether the campus gets built, but which conditions apply, and who can enforce them.

## Stakeholder Landscape

| Stakeholder Group | Primary Domain |
|-------------------|----------------|
| NimbusData (Project Developer) | Capital, timeline, global precedent management |
| Falkenberg Municipal Council | Zoning, local taxes, political survival |
| Citizens' Initiative "Falkenberg First" | Water, transparency, procedural fairness |
| GreenGrid Europe (Environmental NGO) | Binding conditions, climate accounting, systemic precedent |
| Regional Grid Operator | System security, connection assessment, cost recovery |
| Regional Development Agency | Investment attraction, regional business, reputation |
| Farmers & Landowners | Land, water seniority, an internal split between selling and staying |
| State Permitting Authority | Legally defensible permits, binding conditions, political cover |

No single actor can build or block the project alone. The developer holds capital and an exit option. The regulator and grid operator hold hard gates. The civil parties hold endorsement, party status, media attention, and the ballot box. In the real cases, projects died from political cascades and procedural litigation, and survived through conditions that converted opposition into oversight.

## Theoretical Foundations

**Social license to operate.** Thomson and Boutilier (2011) model community acceptance as a pyramid of legitimacy, credibility and trust that is granted, withheld, or withdrawn independently of legal permits. Moffat and Zhang (2014) show empirically that procedural fairness and the quality of contact predict trust better than legal compliance does. A developer can hold every permit and still lose the project.

**Stakeholder salience.** Mitchell, Agle and Wood (1997) explain whose claims get attention as a function of power, legitimacy, and urgency, attributes that shift during a negotiation. Watch how a leaked document or a drought forecast reorders who must be listened to.

**Interests versus positions.** Fisher and Ury (1981): positions are what parties say they want; interests are why. Every role in this simulation holds private interests, constraints and a walk-away alternative that its public position does not reveal.

**Contested numbers as the normal case.** The empirical record on data centers is unsettled. Forecasts within a single reputable source span a threefold range, company disclosures and independent measurements answer different questions, and the field has a documented history of overestimation: the 1999 claim that IT would soon consume half of US electricity was off by a factor of about 25 (Koomey's corrective; Masanet et al., 2020). Efficiency gains, meanwhile, do not automatically reduce totals (Luccioni, Strubell & Crawford, 2025). In this simulation, stakeholders arguing from different, individually defensible numbers is realistic rather than an error. The Key Facts sheet flags which figures are solid and which are contested. Use the flags.

**Environmental justice.** The cloud is not immaterial. Its climate lever sits at the data center, but water stress, toxicity and e-waste burdens sit elsewhere, with fab-adjacent and water-scarce communities along the hardware chain (Velkova, 2016; Lehuedé, 2024). Whether Falkenberg's negotiation should account for burdens Falkenberg will never see is a fair debriefing question.

## The No-Deal Default

Negotiation failure does not preserve the status quo. It triggers a defined default trajectory. If the parties reach no accord:

- NimbusData activates its shortlisted alternative site in another region within months; in the real record, developers walk before losing a long fight
- Falkenberg keeps the dormant industrial zoning, and a poisoned local debate, one year before council elections
- The town's hoped-for tax revenue never materializes, and the Stadtwerke network stays on gas, because no heat deal happens without the anchor heat source
- The Brandt family loses its buyer; the Keller family keeps its farm and its uncertainty
- The regional debate about who pays for grid expansion continues, without the customer who triggered it

This default is the reference point for every party's walk-away calculation. Each role card specifies its own no-deal outcome; rational negotiators accept only agreements that beat it. Note what this default is not: it is not a victory for the project's opponents, and not a catastrophe for the developer. That asymmetry shapes the game.

## Simulation Objectives

This exercise challenges participants to:

1. Negotiate a facility-siting decision inside the European instrument set of binding permit conditions, connection policy and procedural rights, rather than cash-for-acceptance deals
2. Build and hold coalitions under an explicit approval threshold
3. Argue with contested evidence: deploy, challenge, and survive figures that are flagged as conflicting, industry-sourced, or projected
4. Judge every offer against a private walk-away alternative, and recognize when no deal is the rational outcome
5. Experience how social license is built or destroyed, and what transparency, which costs almost nothing, is actually worth
