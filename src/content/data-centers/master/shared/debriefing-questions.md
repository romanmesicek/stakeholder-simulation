# Structured Debriefing Guide

This guide supports the facilitator in leading the debriefing after the Falkenberg simulation. The questions follow the 4-phase model used in experiential learning contexts. Protect this time block; it is where the learning consolidates.

---

## Phase 1: Defusing

*Purpose: Allow participants to process emotions and step out of their roles.*

- How are you feeling right now?
- What was your initial reaction when you received your role, especially if it argued against your personal views?
- What was the most frustrating moment? The most satisfying one?
- Who felt, at some point, that the game was unwinnable for their group?

**Facilitator note:** Let participants vent without judgment. If the accord failed, say explicitly that no deal was a legitimate outcome rather than a group failure. In the real record, comparable projects collapsed too.

---

## Phase 2: Discovery

*Purpose: Surface observations and shared experiences before analysis.*

### Coalition dynamics
- Which alliances formed, and around which of the four topics (water, heat, grid, transparency)?
- Which civil party ended up outside the accord, or closest to it? How did that feel, and what did they do about it?
- Did the two technical gatekeepers (Permitting Authority, Grid Operator) behave like neutral referees or like negotiating parties?

### Power and influence
- Where did power asymmetries become most visible?
- Which group had more influence than its formal position suggested? Watch for the Farmers via the 15 hectares, and the Citizens via party status in the water hearing.
- How did the developer's exit option shape everyone else's behavior? Was the alternative-site threat believed?

### The contested numbers
- Which figure did the most work in the negotiation, and was it a solid one or a flagged one?
- Did anyone successfully challenge a [CONFLICTING] or [INDUSTRY SOURCE] figure? What happened then?
- Did the jobs question (150 announced vs. the 25–40 benchmark) get resolved, exploited, or quietly dropped?

### Negotiation dynamics
- What creative linkages emerged that you didn't anticipate?
- Which commitments got struck for having no funding source?
- Were there breakthrough moments where a position flipped? What triggered them?

---

## Phase 3: Deepening

*Purpose: Connect observations to theoretical frameworks.*

### Social license to operate
- Trace the pyramid: did NimbusData achieve legitimacy? Credibility? Trust? What built or destroyed each level?
- Moffat and Zhang found that procedural fairness and the quality of contact predict acceptance better than legal compliance. Where did the simulation confirm or contradict that?
- The transparency instruments (disclosure, monitoring board) cost the developer almost nothing. Were they offered early, late, or never, and why?

### Walk-away options and coalition arithmetic
- Whose no-deal outcome was genuinely acceptable, and how did that strength show at the table?
- Did any group accept a deal worse than its walk-away option? If so, what pressure produced that?
- How did the approval threshold change behavior compared to a simple majority? Who invested in the cheapest endorsement?

### Regulatory conditionality vs. negotiated benefits
- The European deal space is permit conditions rather than community checks. Where did your accord reflect that, and did anyone try to buy acceptance with cash anyway?
- Which conditions in your package would survive judicial review, and which were legally arbitrary?
- Compare: Ireland ended its moratorium through conditions (on-site generation, 80% new renewables, disclosure). How close did your package come?

### Evidence and forecast humility
- The field's history includes a forecasting error of roughly 25×. How should a regulator set binding conditions when demand projections span +50% to +165%?
- "Efficiency gains reduce impact" versus "cheaper computation drives more consumption" (the Jevons paradox): which story did your negotiation implicitly believe?

### Environmental justice
- Falkenberg negotiated its own water and land. The hardware chain's burdens (fab communities, e-waste, water-stressed regions elsewhere) had no seat. Should they have? Who could have spoken for them?

---

## Phase 4: Transfer (Facilitator-Led Discussion)

*Purpose: Apply learnings to real-world contexts.*

### Against the real record
- Your outcome vs. reality: Kronstorf (pending under protest), Dublin (conditions after a moratorium), Zeewolde (cancelled by political cascade), Espoo (accepted with delivered heat). Which case does your result resemble, and why?
- No real project in the reference record was stopped by a referendum. They were stopped by elections, courts, land control, and connection policy. Does your experience make that pattern plausible?
- What did the simulation oversimplify? Candidates: multi-year timelines compressed to hours, no lawyers, no press cycle, a single municipality instead of competing ones.

### Personal learning
- What surprised you about your own behavior in the role?
- What negotiation move would you make differently, and which one will you keep?
- How did arguing from assigned interests, possibly against your own views, change how you see the public debate about data centers and AI?

### Professional relevance
- Where in your future work will you sit at a table like this, and in which seat?
- What transfers: reading an evidence base with reliability flags, pricing an endorsement, designing conditions that hold?

---

## Facilitator Notes

### Timing guidance
- Defusing ~5 min (don't rush it)
- Discovery ~10 min (the most substantive phase)
- Deepening ~7 min (pick 2–3 blocks that match what actually happened; don't run all of them)
- Transfer ~3 min in plenary; the rest is embedded in the reflection assignment

### Common pitfalls
- Skipping defusing and going straight to analysis
- Re-litigating the accord instead of analyzing the process
- Providing "correct answers" instead of facilitating discovery
- Letting the winning coalition narrate everyone else's experience

### Signs of effective debriefing
- Participants reference specific moments and specific figures
- Insight moments about the cheapness of transparency and the price of ignoring a party
- Connections drawn to the real reference cases without prompting
- Curiosity about the confidential information other groups held
