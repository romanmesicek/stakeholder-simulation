# Common Problems Playbook

Prefer structural fixes over in-the-moment policing. Intervene freely on process (time, turn-taking, calling a caucus); intervene on content only to clear up rule confusion.

## Setup checklist (before the session)

- [ ] Situation briefing and Key Facts sheet assigned as pre-reading; the reliability-flag system only works if students met it before the game
- [ ] Groups of 3–5 per stakeholder; sub-roles assigned (lead negotiator, analyst, note-taker)
- [ ] Mentimeter prepared: interest/power mapping, outcome prediction (strong-conditions accord / weak accord / no accord: project leaves / no accord: proceeds anyway), plus the pre/post questions from the student guide
- [ ] Event cards ready (max 2; E6 and E9 are mutually exclusive closers); 2-minute caucus after each injection
- [ ] Hard stop times announced per phase; the 25-minute debrief block is protected
- [ ] You chair Phases 2 and 6 in the side-role of State Secretary for Digital Infrastructure
- [ ] Remind the two gate roles privately: the Regulator's "not permittable as filed" and the Grid Operator's physics statement should land early, because they organize the whole game
- [ ] Remind NimbusData privately: the exit threat works once, calmly; repeated, it becomes noise

## The problems

### 1. The water number devours the game

**Signs:** Every conversation is 600,000 m³; heat, grid, jobs and land go unnegotiated; positions loop.
**Do:** Enforce the Round 2 strand structure: physically separate tables for water, heat, grid, transparency. Remind the room the accord has four strands and the endorsement arithmetic needs all of them. Water hardliners can stay at the water table; the game continues around them.

### 2. One group dominates everything

**Signs:** One table present in every exchange; others reduced to spectators.
**Do:** Enforce turn order in plenary. The assigned bilaterals guarantee everyone negotiates; hold the pairings. Visit quiet tables during caucuses with one prompt ("What would you need to say yes?"). Rotate the lead-negotiator sub-role within a group.

### 3. A group refuses to play its role

**Signs:** "We wouldn't actually want this"; arguing personal opinions; a NimbusData team playing the villain for laughs, or a Citizens team refusing all engagement.
**Do:** Reframe privately: "You're the delegation, so argue what your principals would argue." Point NimbusData to their constructive cards (heat, jobs guarantee, closed-loop); point Citizens to their conditions wing and the retired judge's counsel. Park ethical objections explicitly for the debrief.

### 4. Consensus arrives suspiciously early

**Signs:** A "deal" before Round 2; no funding sources named; the gates never consulted.
**Do:** Stress-test in role as State Secretary: "Which budget funds the heat pipes? What does the permit condition actually say? Has the Grid Operator seen this?" Send the package to both gates. Early consensus almost always dissolves on the first named number; if it survives, deepen it (drought clause wording, Phase 2 criteria, enforcement).

### 5. Total deadlock

**Signs:** Position repetition; no conditional offers; groups disengaging.
**Do:** Call a 3-minute caucus: "Return with one conditional offer: 'We could accept X if Y.'" Inject E6 (heat grant) to create value that only exists under agreement. Walk the room through the no-deal default group by group; it is asymmetric by design: comfortable for the Regulator and Grid Operator, survivable for Citizens, dangerous for the Agency and Brandt. Naming that asymmetry usually restarts movement.

### 6. The gates misplay their veto

**Signs:** The Regulator rubber-stamps whatever the table likes (or blocks everything to feel powerful); the Grid Operator forgets its physics or wields it as pure obstruction.
**Do:** Privately re-anchor them to their cards: every position must trace to a published criterion or the legal file; refusal requires a concrete, stated reason. Both failure modes are debrief gold: the captured referee and the unaccountable veto player are real regulatory pathologies.

### 7. Confidential information leaks or is weaponized

**Signs:** Brandt's debt or Keller's price revealed early; the Municipality's budget gap named at the table; the Grid Operator's hidden headroom announced.
**Do:** Don't rewind; leaked is leaked, exactly as in reality. Ask the leaking group in passing what they expect the disclosure to buy them, and note the moment for the debrief (information asymmetry is a core teaching point: discovery, disclosure strategy, and their prices). If a leak collapses one party's position entirely, give that group a 2-minute caucus to reset its strategy.

### 8. The 4-of-5 rule becomes a bullying tool

**Signs:** A bloc openly organizes to make one civil party "the excluded one" from the start, usually the Citizens.
**Do:** Let the mechanic work, but make its price visible in role: as State Secretary, ask the mayor on the record how the accord will fare at the election without the initiative, and NimbusData how it fares at the hearing. Exclusion is legitimate strategy *with* a price tag; the game teaches the tag.

### 9. The clock beats the debrief

**Signs:** Round 2 overruns; the conference compresses; debrief shrinks toward zero.
**Do:** Don't allow it. Cut negotiation time, not the debrief: the learning consolidates there, and an unfinished negotiation debriefs just as well as a finished one. The conference needs its full half hour, because the public endorsement count and NimbusData's sign-or-walk moment are the game's payoff.
