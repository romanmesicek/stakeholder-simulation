# Common Problems Playbook

Prefer structural fixes over in-the-moment policing. Intervene freely on **process** (time, turn-taking, calling a caucus); intervene on **content** only to clear up rule confusion.

## Setup checklist (before the session)

- [ ] Situation briefing assigned as pre-reading (the master briefing is substantial — in-session reading time assumes it)
- [ ] Role slips prepared for random draw; groups of 3–5 per stakeholder; sub-roles suggested (lead negotiator, analyst, note-taker)
- [ ] Mentimeter prepared: interest/power mapping (1–10), outcome prediction (Win-win / Partial / Zero-sum / Deadlock), coalition-partner question, plus the pre/post questions from the student guide
- [ ] Event cards ready (max 2, rounds 3–4); 2-minute caucus after each injection
- [ ] Hard stop times announced per phase — the 20-minute debrief block is protected
- [ ] Decide who chairs Phases 2 and 4: you, in the side-role of Regional Energy Minister
- [ ] Feasibility Certificate: remind the Technical Panel privately that certification is their leverage — they should not give it away early

## The problems

### 1. One group dominates everything

**Signs:** One table present in every exchange; others reduced to spectators; airtime concentrated in two voices.
**Do:** Enforce turn order in plenary. Use assigned bilaterals (Rounds 1–2) to guarantee everyone negotiates. Visit quiet tables during caucuses with one prompt ("What would you need to say yes?"). Within a group, rotate the lead-negotiator sub-role.

### 2. A group refuses to play its role

**Signs:** "We wouldn't actually want this"; arguing personal opinions; leaking confidential constraints for effect.
**Do:** Reframe privately: "You're the delegation — argue what your principals would argue." Anchor them to one concrete card element as their next move. Park ethical objections explicitly for the debrief (they're often the best Deepening material).

### 3. Consensus arrives suspiciously early

**Signs:** A "deal" before Round 3; no funding sources named; no one checked the Panel.
**Do:** Stress-test in role: "Which source funds each commitment — government fund, contingency, credit facility?" Send the package to the Technical Panel for certification. Early consensus almost always dissolves on the first named number; if it survives, deepen it (milestones, enforcement, FPIC terms).

### 4. Total deadlock

**Signs:** Position repetition; no conditional offers; groups disengaging.
**Do:** Call a 3-minute caucus: "Return with one conditional offer — 'We could accept X if Y.'" Inject E5 (EU funding window) — it creates value that only exists under agreement. Walk the room through the no-deal default group by group: deadlock is not the status quo, it is the worst ladder rung for almost everyone.

### 5. Escalation gets personal

**Signs:** Interruptions, mockery, bad faith attributed to classmates rather than roles.
**Do:** Interrupt on process: "Attack positions, not people." Separate the parties for one bilateral round. Name it briefly, move on, and bring the underlying interest conflict into Discovery in the debrief.

### 6. The Panel gives its certificate away — or weaponizes it

**Signs:** Technical Panel certifies a vague package to be agreeable, or blocks everything to feel powerful.
**Do:** Privately re-anchor them to their constraints: certify only what they could defend publicly; refusal requires a concrete, stated risk. Both failure modes are debrief gold (the captured expert / the unaccountable veto player).

### 7. The clock beats the debrief

**Signs:** Round 5 overruns; debrief shrinks toward zero.
**Do:** Don't allow it. Cut Round 5 short instead — Crookall's rule applies: the learning consolidates in the debrief, not in the last ten minutes of bargaining. An unfinished negotiation debriefs just as well as a finished one.
