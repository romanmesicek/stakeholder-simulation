# Research Prompt: Data Center Controversy — Stakeholder Simulation Scenario

Status: ready to hand to an external research agent (August 2026).
Purpose: gather the raw material from which we will distill a new scenario
("data-centers") for the stakeholder-simulation platform. Decisions behind this
prompt are documented at the end of this file.

---

## The prompt (copy everything below this line)

You are a research agent preparing the factual and didactic foundation for an
educational negotiation simulation (role-play) used in university teaching at
bachelor level. In the simulation, 6–8 student groups take on stakeholder roles
and negotiate whether — and under what conditions — a hyperscale data center
campus gets built in a fictional mid-sized European municipality. The scenario
is fictional but must be distilled from real, well-documented cases. The
simulation language is English.

Your job is NOT to write the simulation itself. Your job is to produce a
comprehensive, well-sourced research dossier covering the eight blocks below.
A scenario author will later turn your dossier into role cards, briefings, and
facilitator material.

### Ground rules

- **Steelman both sides.** Public reporting on data centers skews critical
  (water, electricity, "no jobs"). A good negotiation game requires every side
  to hold a seriously playable hand. Research the strongest evidence-based
  arguments FOR data center development too: fiscal revenue (with real
  figures), digital sovereignty and latency arguments, efficiency progress
  (PUE trends), hyperscalers' 24/7 carbon-free energy procurement, waste-heat
  reuse as genuine community benefit. For every controversy dimension, state
  explicitly where the criticism is empirically solid and where it is
  exaggerated or outdated.
- **Cite everything.** Every factual claim carries an inline source (publisher,
  year, URL). Academic sources as full APA 7 citations. Prefer material from
  2022 onwards (the AI-boom era); older sources only for foundational concepts.
- **Distinguish fact from position.** When you report a stakeholder's claim,
  label it as their position, not as established fact.
- **European frame first.** The fictional scenario is set in a generic EU
  country: EU permitting logic, the Energy Efficiency Directive (waste-heat
  obligations for data centers), grid-connection queues. US cases serve as
  contrast material, not as the institutional template.

### Block 1 — Reference cases (5–7 real conflicts)

Document 5–7 real data center siting conflicts. Three are mandatory:

1. **Kronstorf, Upper Austria** — the currently debated hyperscale project;
   cover energy demand, land use, municipal politics, and the state of the
   public debate.
2. **Dublin / Ireland** — grid capacity crisis and the de-facto connection
   moratorium; data centers' share of national electricity demand.
3. **Zeewolde, Netherlands** — the Meta hyperscale project and its collapse;
   national vs. municipal politics, farmland, the "what do we get?" debate.

Choose 2–4 further cases yourself for maximum contrast (candidates: Frankfurt
am Main, a Nordic waste-heat success case such as in Denmark or Finland,
Loudoun County/Virginia as US contrast, a water-stressed case such as
Aragón/Spain or Chile/Uruguay). For each case report: project size (MW, ha,
investment), key actors, timeline of the conflict, the decisive turning
points, outcome (built / modified / cancelled / pending), and sources.

### Block 2 — Controversy map

Map the conflict dimensions systematically. For each dimension give real
number ranges with sources, plus the strongest argument on each side and an
honest verdict on the empirical state of the debate:

- Electricity demand and grid capacity (incl. who pays for grid expansion)
- Water consumption and cooling technologies
- Land use / soil sealing / farmland conversion
- Jobs: construction vs. permanent operations jobs — promises vs. evidence
- Fiscal effects: taxes, subsidies, tax abatements — who gains what
- Waste heat: technical potential, real projects, economics
- Noise, landscape, traffic during construction and operation
- The AI boom as demand driver: growth projections and their uncertainty
- Climate commitments of hyperscalers (24/7 CFE, PPAs) vs. actual grid impact

### Block 3 — Stakeholder profiles (8–10)

Profile 8–10 stakeholder groups. Six are fixed (they will definitely be roles
in the simulation); propose 2–4 more based on what the real cases show:

Fixed: (1) the hyperscaler / project developer, (2) the municipal council and
mayor, (3) a residents' citizens initiative, (4) an environmental NGO with a
supra-regional perspective, (5) the grid operator / energy utility, (6) the
regional business community / economic development agency.

Candidates to evaluate: farmers/landowners, the state-level permitting
authority, construction unions, academic/technical experts.

For each group: core interests, red lines, negotiation leverage (what can they
actually block or offer?), typical arguments — each backed by real positions,
statements, or quotes from the reference cases.

### Block 4 — Negotiation dynamics

What deals happened in reality? Collect documented examples of: community
benefit agreements and funds, waste-heat delivery commitments, binding
efficiency/water conditions in permits, moratoria and their political
mechanics, projects that failed and why, projects that succeeded and what
concessions made them acceptable. Also: which coalitions formed between which
actors, and what unexpected alliances occurred. This block becomes the raw
material for the simulation's negotiation logic and event cards.

### Block 5 — Key facts sheet

15–20 hard, citable numbers a student can use in negotiation: typical MW and
water figures per hyperscale campus, jobs per MW benchmarks, PUE ranges,
data-center share of electricity demand in Ireland vs. EU average, typical
investment volumes and municipal tax revenues, waste-heat potential figures.
Each with source and year, formatted so a scenario author can lift them
directly into a one-page reference sheet.

### Block 6 — Academic literature (8–12 sources)

8–12 peer-reviewed sources on: data center siting conflicts and community
acceptance, energy/water footprint of computing and AI, environmental justice
dimensions of digital infrastructure, social license to operate. Full APA 7
citations, 2–3 sentence annotation each on what the source contributes to the
scenario or the debriefing discussion.

### Block 7 — Best practices: role-play and simulation design

Current research and practice on designing negotiation simulations for higher
education: role card design (length, structure, confidential vs. public
information), information asymmetry as a design tool, scoring and success
criteria, event cards / external shocks, single-session vs. multi-round
formats. Include established exemplars (e.g. Harvard PON teaching simulations,
climate negotiation games such as the World Climate Simulation) and what makes
them work.

### Block 8 — Best practices: running role-plays in class

Current evidence on facilitation: briefing and role-assignment techniques,
phasing and timing for a 3–4 hour session, handling dominant and passive
participants, when and how facilitators should intervene, debriefing methods
(structured reflection models, connecting experience to theory), typical
failure modes of classroom simulations and how to prevent them.

### Output format

One coherent Markdown report, structured exactly along Blocks 1–8, in English.
Length: whatever thoroughness requires — expect roughly 6,000–10,000 words.
Inline citations throughout; APA 7 reference list for academic sources at the
end. Do not invent facts; where evidence is thin or contested, say so.

## Decisions behind this prompt (internal, not part of the prompt)

Agreed 2026-08-10 (grill-me session):

- **Approach:** fictional municipality, distilled from real reference cases
  (Kronstorf/AT explicitly included as culturally close case).
- **Setting:** generic-European (EU institutional frame), simulation in English.
- **Levels:** research at full complexity (8–10 stakeholders); bachelor
  implementation first with 6–7 groups selected during distillation
  ("option C"). Master level remains a documented option.
- **Core stakeholder set (fixed):** hyperscaler, municipality, citizens
  initiative, environmental NGO, grid operator, regional business — final
  selection of additional groups happens at distillation time.
- **Blocks 7–8** feed directly into content-review packages 2 (negotiation
  logic) and 3 (didactics & facilitator material).
- **Execution:** the prompt runs on the user's external research agent
  (separate server, background operation), not locally. The resulting report
  will be stored in this repo (suggested: `docs/research-data-centers.md`)
  and distilled from there.
